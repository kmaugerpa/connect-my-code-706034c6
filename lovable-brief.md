## PAGE 7: THE BETS (/bets)

### Header
- H1: `Thirty years.` (line break) `Ten bets I'd make again.` (italic)
- Intro: Every one of these cost me something to learn — a deal, a relationship, a year, sometimes more. Not borrowed frameworks or theory. Principles tested under real pressure, built into how I lead and how I work. The ones that gave the most back were usually the ones I was least certain about at the start.

### 10 Bet Cards (vertical list, numbered)
Each: large serif number left column (with right border) + title + body paragraph + italic anchor story

01 **Begin with the end in mind.**
Body: If you don't know what success looks like, you're navigating without a destination. The clearest thing I can do as a leader — or as a board member — is define what we're actually trying to build, and make sure everyone in the room is looking at the same picture.
Anchor: When I bought NCC, I had a vision of what it could be. That vision was the compass through twelve years of noise.

02 **Hope is an underrated virtue.**
Body: Hope is the predecessor to belief, and belief is where strategy begins. The leaders who stripped hope out of their vocabulary in favor of pure analytical rigor tended to build precise, efficient, uninspiring organizations.
Anchor: Every one of my biggest bets — NCC, the ESOP, Flexmove Americas — started with hope long before it started with a spreadsheet.

03 **Find the good. Live there.**
Body: What you choose to focus on is not just a mindset — it's a leadership decision with downstream consequences.
Anchor: I told my team this directly, many times: what you focus on determines the quality of what you build.

04 **The customer is the north star.**
Body: Not a slogan. An operating principle. Every strategic question eventually comes back to it.
Anchor: We built a category-leading position in the ophthalmic industry because we asked what customers couldn't get elsewhere — and then built exactly that.

05 **People buy in to what they help create.**
Body: Directives get compliance. Involvement gets ownership.
Anchor: The ESOP didn't create our ownership culture. It formalized one that already existed — because we'd been practicing this for years.

06 **Assume best intent.**
Body: I was a chronic blame-finder early in my career. The shift from blame to curiosity is one of the highest-leverage moves a leader can make.
Anchor: I haven't written a punishing email in twenty years. That one change bought me more goodwill and better information than almost anything else I did.

07 **Business is personal. Always.**
Body: "It's not personal, it's business" is usually code for avoiding accountability. Business is made of people.
Anchor: Every number on a financial statement is a story about a person. I never forgot that — and neither did my team.

08 **Ask more than you answer.**
Body: The best leaders aren't the ones with the most answers — they're the ones who ask the best questions.
Anchor: We almost sank a product launch because we assumed we knew what customers would pay. We didn't ask. Curiosity is cheaper than failure.

09 **Be concise.**
Body: The more words, the less effect. Clarity is a form of respect — for the listener's time and for the idea itself.
Anchor: I wrote a poem about this once. It was short.

10 **Set people up. Own it when it goes wrong.**
Body: Before you conclude someone failed, ask whether they were set up to succeed. Most underperformance is a systems problem dressed up as a people problem.
Anchor: I gave three last chance letters in my career. Two worked. One didn't. In every case, I asked myself first — did I do everything I could to set this person up?
